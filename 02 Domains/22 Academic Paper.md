# 22 Academic Paper

An overlay with two modes. Decide which at intake — they run differently.

---

## Mode A — Exploration (mapping a field)

**Goal:** know what is established, what is contested, and where the actual open question is.

### Method

1. **Find the anchor.** The two or three works everyone in the area cites. Read them properly, not their abstracts.
2. **Trace forward.** Follow citations *from* the anchors to the present. Backward citation gives history; forward citation gives the live debate.
3. **Find the disagreement.** Where do competent people in this field disagree, and about what — the finding, the method, or the interpretation? This is where the open question lives, not in the "future work" sections.
4. **Check whether it held.** Replication status, meta-analyses, retractions, whether the effect survived larger samples or preregistration. A famous result is not a durable one.
5. **Map the schools.** Who works in which tradition, and what does each take for granted? Consensus and a loud subfield look identical from outside.

### Evidence discipline

- **Preprint vs peer-reviewed** is recorded every time.
- **Read before citing.** Abstract and method at minimum. A paper cited from another paper's description of it is second-hand and marked as such.
- Note **sample, power, and preregistration**. Effect sizes with intervals over p-values. An underpowered significant result is weak evidence, not strong.
- Field-specific base rates: know roughly how much of this literature replicates.

### The gap is usually not the stated gap

Papers name future work that is convenient for them. The real open question is generally at the seam where two literatures touch and do not talk, or where a method's assumption has never been tested. Look there.

## Mode B — Construction (writing a paper)

### Order of operations

1. **Contribution, in one sentence.** *"This paper shows X, which was not previously known / was assumed without test."* If this sentence cannot be written, there is no paper yet — return to Mode A.
2. **Claim structure.** The chain of claims from premises to contribution. Each one gets a line.
3. **Evidence requirement per claim.** What must be shown, and is it obtainable? This is where infeasible papers get caught, cheaply.
4. **Limitations — written now, not last.** Drafting limitations early disciplines the claims; written last, they become apologies for overreach already committed.
5. **Then structure**, then prose.

### Citation discipline — non-negotiable

- **Never fabricate a citation.** No invented authors, titles, years, journals, DOIs, or page numbers. Not even as a placeholder — placeholders survive into drafts.
- Every citation traces to a real work actually consulted. If it could not be located, write `[CITATION NEEDED — searched: <terms>]` and leave it visible.
- Quote exactly, with a locator. Paraphrase is marked as paraphrase.
- Never cite for a claim the source does not make. Citation drift — a source cited for a stronger claim than it supports — is the most common integrity failure in synthesis work.

### Structure conventions

Follow the target venue's, and say which one. Absent a target: contribution → related work positioning the contribution → method → results → limitations → implications. Related work positions the paper; it is not a bibliography with sentences.

---

## Typical deliverable

- Mode A: a structured review memo — established / contested / open — with a full reference list, and each reference tiered.
- Mode B: an annotated outline first, then prose. `.docx` via the **docx** skill when the venue or collaborators need it; markdown otherwise. Keep the reference list as its own file in the project folder from the start.
