# 21 Business and Market

An overlay. The protocol still runs; this adds what commercial research specifically requires.

## The three questions

Most business research reduces to these. Decide at intake which one is actually being asked — they need different evidence.

1. **Is the market real?** — does the demand exist, is it budgeted, is it growing
2. **Is the position defensible?** — can this be taken, and can it be held
3. **Can it be reached economically?** — does the go-to-market math work

## Market sizing

**Vendor TAM figures are D-tier until decomposed.** A number from a firm that sells research into a market, or a company raising money against it, is marketing until you can see the method.

Always build **bottom-up**: number of potential buyers × realistic penetration × realistic price. Show every input and its source. State the assumption that carries the most weight.

Use top-down only as a sanity check on the bottom-up number, and say when they disagree — a factor-of-ten gap between the two is itself the finding.

Separate:
- **TAM** — everyone who could theoretically buy
- **SAM** — those this offering can actually serve today
- **SOM** — those reachable through existing channels within the horizon

Most "TAM" figures in circulation are SAM at best. Deflate accordingly and say that you did.

## Competitive landscape

A logo grid is not analysis. Establish:

- **Who actually wins deals, and on what basis** — price, integration, incumbency, compliance, switching cost
- **Where the buyer's money comes from now** — the true competitor is usually the incumbent process or a spreadsheet, not the named rival
- **Concentration and its trend** — consolidating or fragmenting
- **Recent movement** — named accounts won or lost in the last 24 months, price changes, entries and exits

## The customer

The job being hired for, in the buyer's words, not the seller's. Sources: reviews, forums, job postings (what tools they list, what they are hiring to fix), support and community channels, disclosed case studies read sceptically, procurement documents where public.

Distinguish the **user**, the **buyer**, and the **budget holder**. They are frequently three people with different problems, and a product loved by users and unfunded by buyers is a specific and common failure mode.

## Commercial economics

Where available: acquisition cost, payback period, retention and net revenue retention, gross margin by segment, sales cycle length. Where not disclosed, say so and reason from proxies — headcount mix, disclosed segment splits, hiring patterns — clearly labelled as inference.

## Structural constraints

Regulation, licensing, certification, procurement cycles, data residency, union or professional-body gatekeeping. These decide more market outcomes than product quality and are consistently underweighted because they are boring.

## Interest flags

For every figure, name who benefits from it being large. Market sizes, growth rates, adoption statistics, and survey results overwhelmingly originate with parties who profit from optimism. This does not make them wrong; it makes them tiered and flagged. See `03 Standards/30 Source Grading.md`.

## Typical deliverable

A memo, plus a sizing sheet (**xlsx**) with assumptions as editable inputs so the reader can test their own. A deck only when it will be presented — and then the memo comes first, since the deck is a compression of it.
